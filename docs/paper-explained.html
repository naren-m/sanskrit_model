Neural proposes,
Pāṇini disposes

How a 10.7-million-parameter model the size of a rounding error, wrapped in a 2,400-year-old grammar, taught us that the bottleneck in machine Sanskrit was never the model — it was the shape we forced it into.

रामः + अस्ति  →  रामोऽस्ति rāmaḥ + asti → rāmo'sti · two words fuse into one. Reading Sanskrit means running this backwards.

This page explains an entire research project, and the paper that came out of it, assuming you know nothing about Sanskrit and nothing about the machinery of language models. We will build up from the single hardest fact about written Sanskrit, arrive at a surprising experimental result, and end on a lesson that has little to do with Sanskrit at all: when a system underperforms, fix its architecture before you reach for more compute.

Why this problem at all? Sanskrit has a vast digitized corpus — epics, philosophy, science — locked behind a preprocessing wall. Machines can't search or translate what they can't first segment into words.

Everything below is measured, not asserted. Every number comes from running the code on held-out text. Where the model failed, the page says so and shows the failure.

§ 1The problem: a language written without spaces, that also melts its own seams

Two facts about classical Sanskrit, stacked, create the whole difficulty.

First: no spaces. Sanskrit was a recited, memorized tradition long before a written one. Text runs continuously — रामोऽस्तिवनम् — with no gaps to mark where one word ends and the next begins. That alone is a segmentation problem, like reading theskyisblue.

Second, and worse: sandhi. At the junction where two words meet, their sounds physically fuse and change, according to strict phonological rules. This is not optional and not cosmetic — it rewrites the characters at the seam.

sandhi (संधि) literally "putting together." The euphonic transformation at a sound boundary. English does a faint version — "a" becomes "an" before a vowel — but Sanskrit does it everywhere, obligatorily, and it deletes and merges letters.
रामःrāmaḥ ("Rāma") + अस्तिasti ("is") → रामोऽस्तिrāmo'sti

Look at the seam. rāmaḥ ended in a -aḥ (a visarga); asti began with a-. The two collided and became -o'-. The letters that told you where the boundary was have dissolved into new letters. To recover the two original words, a reader must run the fusion backwards — and that reverse is ambiguous, because several different word-pairs can fuse to the same surface string.

So the task, called sandhi splitting or word segmentation, is: given the fused, space-free stream, recover the sequence of original words. It is the gateway to every downstream use — search, translation, grammatical analysis. Get this wrong and everything after it is wrong.

§ 2Pāṇini: a formal generative grammar, from the 4th century BCE

Here is the twist that makes Sanskrit special for computer science. The rules that govern this fusion are not folklore — they were formalized, exhaustively, around 350 BCE by the grammarian Pāṇini in a work of roughly 4,000 terse rules called the Aṣṭādhyāyī. It is, in a real sense, the oldest surviving generative grammar: a system of ordered rewrite rules with a metalanguage, recursion, and abbreviation conventions that computer scientists recognize instantly.

Why a grammarian matters to an ML project. Pāṇini's rules give us a ground truth generator. We can apply sandhi forward with certainty. The hard direction is undoing it — and that's exactly where a lattice of the rules, run in reverse, helps.

This project encodes a large slice of that grammar as executable data. The symbolic core has three parts:

Nothing is asserted the engine cannot re-derive. "Neural proposes, Pāṇini disposes."

That last engine is worth a detour, because it shows the symbolic side working perfectly. Give it a verse and it identifies the metre from first principles — counting guru (heavy) and laghu (light) syllables and matching the pattern.

किमेकं दैवतं लोके किं वाप्येकं परायणम् … Opening of the Viṣṇu Sahasranāma. The engine scans it as anuṣṭubh (pathyā) — the 32-syllable śloka — obeying the 5th-light, 6th-heavy law in every quarter. Pure rules, no neural net involved.

The metre engine even catches the subtle cases: fed a verse whose third quarter breaks the standard pattern, it can be extended to recognize a vipulā — a licensed variant — rather than mislabeling the verse. The symbolic layer, on its home turf, is exact. Hold that thought: exactness is the symbolic layer's whole value, and also its trap.

§ 3The neural approach: a tiny model that learns the statistics

The opposite strategy makes no reference to rules. Take a plain character-level transformer — a GPT, the same architecture as the large ones, just minuscule — and train it to predict the next character of Sanskrit text. It learns, from exposure alone, what fused strings tend to split into.

SLP1 is a 1-character = 1-phoneme ASCII scheme for Sanskrit. Because sandhi and morphology are character phenomena, a character-level vocabulary is the natural unit — no subword guessing.

For scale: published neural Sanskrit segmenters are on the order of 300 million parameters and train for GPU-days on large corpora. This model is roughly 1/30,000th of that training cost. That frugality is deliberate — it is the whole point of pairing it with the grammar. The question the project set out to answer was: how small can the neural part be if the symbolic part does the heavy lifting?

But the neural approach has a failure mode the grammar does not: it can hallucinate. A statistical model, asked to produce output, will happily emit a fluent, plausible, wrong segmentation — one that doesn't even reconstruct back into the original input. That is unacceptable for a tool meant to preprocess a sacred, exact textual tradition.

§ 4The hybrid: four layers, and one guarantee

So combine them. The design — the "standard" neuro-symbolic prescription — reads left to right:

1 · Lattice
propose
The sandhi rules, run in reverse, enumerate every segmentation the grammar permits. Exhaustive, never illegal — but over-generates.
2 · Lexicon
gate
85,715 real corpus words. A split into attested words beats one into non-words.
3 · Model
rank
The neural net scores the survivors and picks the best. It ranks; it does not invent.
4 · Verify
dispose
The chosen split is round-tripped through the rules. If it doesn't re-derive the input, it's rejected.

The intended division of labor is elegant: the grammar guarantees legality, the lexicon supplies real-word bias, and the neural net contributes the one thing rules and dictionaries lack — judgment about which legal, lexical option is actually right in context. Everyone assumed the model was carrying that step.

The experiment

To find out what the model actually contributes, hold the candidate pool fixed and swap out only the ranker. If the neural ranker is doing real work, it should beat a dumb baseline. If it isn't, it will tie one.

§ 5The result that reframed the project

Four rankers, scored on the same 400 held-out lines, each choosing from the identical set of grammar-proposed candidates. The only thing that varies is who does the ranking.

Read the top three rows. The neural ranker scores 6.8%. A scorer with no learned component at all — just "prefer splits into frequent dictionary words" — scores 6.8%. They are indistinguishable. The expensive, trained model, in the role the architecture assigned it, adds nothing.

The oracle ceiling. 6.8% is the fraction of lines whose correct answer even exists anywhere in the grammar's candidate list. No ranker — however clever — can select an option that was never proposed. All three rankers are pinned against this wall.

Every ranker is capped at 6.8% because that is the ceiling of what the grammar proposed in the first place. The bottleneck was never the ranking. It was proposal — the candidates the lattice could generate at all.

Now change the model's job. Instead of ranking a fixed menu, let the same weights freely generate the segmentation, character by character, unconstrained by the lattice:

Six times the accuracy. The identical model, freed from the lattice, reaches 39.8% exact — because it can place a word boundary the grammar's rules structurally cannot propose. The knowledge was in the parameters all along. The architecture was throwing it away.

But — the catch

30% of the free model's outputs are not re-derivable: concatenating its predicted words does not reproduce the input. This is the hallucination the symbolic gate existed to prevent. Coverage bought at the cost of the guarantee.

§ 6Why the grammar is blind — and it's not fixable with more rules

Why can't the lattice propose 93% of the right answers? Because of one measured fact about how Sanskrit words actually abut.

76.4%
of real word-boundaries have no sandhi change — the words simply touch, unchanged
12.6×
more likely the grammar holds the answer when a real transform did occur
91.8%
of the free model's wins are on lines the grammar provably cannot reach

The lattice works by spotting the signature a fusion leaves in the string, then undoing it. But three out of four real boundaries leave no signature at all — the two words just sit next to each other, unchanged (like vana + idam where nothing melts). With no fusion to detect, the rule engine has nothing to key on, and cannot propose a boundary there. It is, precisely, a sandhi-transform detector — excellent when a transform happened, blind when one didn't.

"So just add identity rules!" Tempting, and wrong. An identity rule for every letter-pair would license a boundary at nearly every character — over 11× more candidate cut-points than there are real boundaries. That doesn't solve segmentation; it just moves the impossible job back onto the ranker, which we already saw tops out at 6.8%.

This is the deep finding, and the source of the project's title claim. Capacity was never the constraint. The 10.7M-parameter model already knows the boundaries — 91.8% of its correct answers are ones the grammar's whole apparatus could not even offer as options. The rank-over-a-lattice shape of the system was discarding knowledge the weights already held. The fix had to be architectural, not a bigger model and not more rules.

§ 7The synthesis: let it generate, but forbid it to lie

The free generator had the coverage; it lacked the guarantee. So constrain the generation — but with the right constraint. Not "only propose lattice-legal boundaries" (that recreates the 6.8% ceiling). Instead: copy-constrained decoding.

The decoder must replay the input's characters verbatim, in order. Its only freedom is where to insert a boundary between them. It can never change, drop, or invent a character. Re-derivability becomes 100% — by construction. The output cannot fail to reconstruct the input, because it is the input plus separators.

This is the best of both: the model's learned judgment about where words break, fenced inside a hard structural promise it physically cannot violate. The result:

Forcing the model to keep every character didn't cost accuracy — it slightly improved it, because when the model would have corrupted a character, putting the correct one back into its context repairs the rest of the decode. The constraint is not a tax; it is regularization.

§ 8The one honest gap — and why it finally points at data

We predicted, before building it, that the constrained decoder could reach 47.9%. It reached 40.8% — a 7.1-point shortfall. Rather than wave it away, the project chased it, and the chase is itself instructive.

Two suspects for the gap were tested. Maybe the model's greedy "cut here?" decision was miscalibrated — so we swept a threshold across seven settings. It peaked exactly at the shipped setting; every adjustment made it worse. Maybe it needed a wider search — so we ran a beam. It bought +1 point at 10× the cost. Neither the decision rule nor the search was the problem.

The decomposition. Split the lines by whether the free model had corrupted them. On "clean" lines the constrained decoder hits 47.9% — the prediction, exactly. On "hard" lines it hits 24.2%. The entire shortfall lives in the hard lines.

The decomposition told the real story. On the lines the model handled cleanly, it hit the predicted 47.9% precisely. The shortfall was entirely on the hard lines — where the model both corrupts characters and misplaces boundaries, two symptoms of the same under-modeling. Character corruption and boundary confusion co-occur because those lines are genuinely harder to model.

Architecture first, then capacity

This is the only point in the whole investigation where "train a better model" is the right answer — and it arrives after the architecture is fixed, narrowly, for the hard subset. Had we scaled the model inside the old rank-over-lattice design, we'd have bought nothing: the ranker was pinned at a ceiling no capacity could lift.

§ 9What is actually worth publishing here

The accuracy numbers (6.8% – 40.8%) are far below leaderboard Sanskrit segmenters, and deliberately so — this is a from-scratch, single-corpus, five-minute model built to isolate an architectural question, not to win a benchmark. The contributions are methodological.

A

A reusable ablation method

Hold the candidate pool fixed and swap only the scorer; then remove the pool and let the same weights generate. This cleanly separates "does the model know?" from "can the architecture express what it knows?"

B

Re-derivability as an anti-hallucination metric

A headline number — does the answer reconstruct the input? — that measures faithfulness structurally, connecting to the broader hallucination literature. Copy-constraint drives it to 100% by construction rather than by filtering.

C

Compute-frugality as a finding, not a footnote

A 10.7M model at ~1/30,000th the training cost of a 300M baseline, made viable by the symbolic scaffold — with a measured account of exactly where the small model suffices and where it doesn't.

And the transferable lesson, the one that outlives Sanskrit: a negative result on the ranker plus a positive result on the generator, from the same weights, is a precise instrument. It tells you the deficiency is in the system's shape, not its scale — and it tells you that before you spend a single GPU-day on a bigger model.
