Teaching a Tiny Neural Network Sanskrit — and Never Trusting It

“Neural proposes, Pāṇini disposes.”

A first-principles tour of this project: why Sanskrit is a uniquely good target for a hybrid neural + symbolic system, what we built, what the numbers say, what is broken, and whether there is a paper in it. Written for a reader who knows neither Sanskrit grammar nor machine learning — each is explained from zero.

Chapter 1 · The ProblemWhy is Sanskrit hard for a computer?

Take the English sentence “Rama is here.” A computer can split it into words by looking for spaces. Sanskrit refuses to give you that. When words meet, their boundary sounds fuse by mandatory phonetic rules called sandhi (“junction”). The two words:

rāma  +  asti      (“Rama” + “is”)

are not written as two words. The final a and initial a merge into a long ā, and the text you actually see is:

rāmāsti            (one unbroken string)

Every classical text — the Rāmāyaṇa, the Bhagavad-Gītā — is written this way: a continuous stream of fused syllables. To do anything useful (search, translate, build a dictionary lookup, teach a student), a machine must first solve three problems:

ProblemSanskrit nameWhat it means
Segmentationsandhi-viccheda Undo the fusion: rāmāsti → rāma + asti. Ambiguous — several splits are often phonetically legal.
Morphologyvyākaraṇa For each word, find its root and grammatical features: gacchati → root gam (“to go”), present tense, 3rd person singular.
Meterchandas Verse follows strict light/heavy syllable patterns (145+ named meters). Identifying the meter validates and dates a verse.

The killer difficulty is ambiguity. rāmāsti could in principle split several ways that are all phonetically legal; only one yields real words in a sensible sentence. Undoing sandhi is like un-mixing paint — you need to know what colors existed before they were combined. That “knowing” is exactly what this project is about.

Chapter 2 · The Symbolic HalfPāṇini: a compiler spec from 500 BCE

Here is what makes Sanskrit unique among all human languages: around the 5th century BCE, the grammarian Pāṇini wrote the Aṣṭādhyāyī — roughly 4,000 terse rules (sūtras) that generate the entire language mechanically. Given a root and desired features, the rules derive the correct surface word step by step, like a compiler. It is the oldest known formal generative grammar — predating Chomsky by 2,400 years — and it is complete enough to execute in software.

This means Sanskrit is the one natural language where you can build a verifier: a program that, handed a claimed analysis (“gacchati comes from root gam, class 1, present tense”), can re-derive the word from scratch and check the claim. Not “probably right” — provably right or provably wrong.

The project's symbolic core lives in slm/rules.py (pure Python, no dependencies), loaded from machine-readable tables:

AssetRowsEngineDoes
sandhi-rules-full.csv~1,468 SandhiEngineJoin two words correctly; enumerate every legal way to split a fused string.
dhatus-full.csv2,259 DhatuKoshaThe Dhātupāṭha — Pāṇini's catalogue of verb roots with class + meaning. The ground truth for verifying root claims.
meters-full.csv145 ChandasEngineNamed meters as light/heavy syllable patterns; identifies a verse's meter.
Why not symbolic-only, then?

Because rules over-generate. The sandhi rules can tell you the 20 splits of a string that are phonetically legal; they cannot tell you which one a human meant. Ranking by plausibility is a statistical judgment — exactly what rules are worst at and neural networks are best at.

Chapter 3 · The Neural HalfWhat the network is, from zero

Character-level language model

The neural component is a GPT — the same architecture family as ChatGPT, but microscopic: 10.7 million parameters (ChatGPT-class models are ~100,000× larger). It does one thing: read a sequence of characters and predict the next character, over and over. That's the entire trick. “Understanding” emerges because predicting the next character of Sanskrit well forces the network to internalize spelling, word shapes, and sandhi statistics.

SLP1: one letter, one sound

We don't feed it Devanagari script (each glyph is 3 UTF-8 bytes and vowels attach as diacritics — a mess). Everything is transliterated to SLP1, an ASCII scheme where one character = exactly one phoneme: धर्म → Darma. Since sandhi and morphology are sound-level phenomena, this gives the model the cleanest possible view — a vocabulary of only ~120 symbols instead of the 50,000+ word-pieces English models use.

One model, five skills

Every task is disguised as the same next-character game, using a control token to say which skill is wanted (loss is computed only on the answer span):

TaskInput → outputTraining data source
segfused text → pada1 | pada2Rāmāyaṇa, 33k verses with gold word order (~308k words)
morphroot → class + meaningDhātupāṭha (deterministic)
sandhiword + word → fused formrules applied forward over real vocabulary — free unlimited data
meterlight/heavy pattern → meter namechandas table
denoisetext with masked spans → the spansYoga Sūtras + joined Rāmāyaṇa
The headline metric: val_bpb

Bits per byte on held-out text: how many bits the model needs, on average, to encode each character it has never seen. Random guessing over our alphabet ≈ 7 bits. Our model: ~0.46. Compression is understanding — you can only compress what you can predict, and you can only predict Sanskrit characters by knowing Sanskrit. Training runs in ~5 minutes on a Mac, which is the point: the loop is fast enough to actually experiment (the autoresearch discipline — one editable train.py, fixed wall-clock budget, one metric).

Chapter 4 · The KeystoneFour layers: why a tiny model suffices

Neither half works alone. Rules can't rank; a 10.7M network hallucinates — it will cheerfully invent a root that doesn't exist, because it predicts plausible characters, not truth. The architecture (slm/pipeline.py) locks them together so each covers the other's failure mode:

Symbolic

1 · Lattice — enumerate

SandhiEngine.split lists every rule-licensed segmentation. Exhaustive, never wrong about what is legal — but over-generates and cannot rank.

▼ candidates
Symbolic

2 · Lexicon — gate

Real corpus vocabulary filters: a split whose words are attested in 33k verses beats one that isn't. A hard out-of-vocabulary penalty stops the model's love of copying its input.

▼ survivors
Neural

3 · Model — rank

The GPT scores each survivor's plausibility (Inference.logprob) and contributes its own guess as one more candidate. It proposes and ranks — it never invents characters outside the lattice.

▼ best analysis
Symbolic

4 · Verify — certify

Each chosen word is round-tripped through the Dhātupāṭha. Nothing is asserted that the rule engine cannot independently confirm. A hallucinated root (morph zzzq) is rejected, visibly.

The ranking formula makes the power balance explicit — the symbolic lexicon signal dominates and the neural score only breaks ties:

score = 4.0·lex_coverage − 0.6·oov_count + 0.3·model_logprob − 0.05·n_words
The economic insight

Published Sanskrit systems throw 300M–600M-parameter models at these tasks. Because our model is only ever a ranker inside a symbolically constrained space — never a free generator — the hard guarantees come from the rules, and the network only needs enough capacity to have taste. That is why 10.7M parameters and five minutes of training produce usable behavior. Whether that trade-off holds up under rigorous evaluation is the project's central research question.

The classic textbook sandhi cases all come out right: rAma+asti → rAmAsti, tat+hitam → tadDitam, deva+indra → devendra, gaNgA+udakam → gaNgodakam. Chapter 6 · Honest LedgerWhat's broken or missing

Issue 1 — segmentation is the weak link (38.5%)

Exact-match means the whole sentence must be split perfectly — a harsh metric — but state-of-the-art systems reach 85%+ on comparable tasks. Root causes: the model decodes greedily and unconstrained (the lattice-constrained decoding that would forbid illegal splits is designed but not yet wired into the seg decoder), and the training corpus is narrow.

Issue 2 — morphology of inflected words is a stub

Today we verify bare roots only. Given gacchati (an inflected surface form), the pipeline cannot yet say “root gam, present, 3rd singular” — that needs millions of (form → analysis) training pairs, which the vidyut-prakriya derivation engine can generate exhaustively. Designed (spec §2.1), not built. The pipeline reports this honestly as pending rather than faking it.

Issue 3 — the go/no-go baseline hasn't been run

The spec's own discipline: the neural model earns its place only if it beats a dumb trigram baseline on verification-survival and segmentation. That baseline isn't built yet, so “the network helps” is currently a hypothesis, not a measurement. This matters doubly because the current scoring formula weights the symbolic lexicon 13× over the model — the ablation may reveal the model contributes little at this size.

Issue 4 — evaluation is in-distribution and non-standard

The 91–96% sandhi number is measured on the same rule-generated distribution the model trained on. Nothing has been run on community benchmarks (the SIGHUM / Hackathon sandhi-split datasets, DCS held-out works), so no number is comparable to published literature yet. Corpus breadth is also thin: two texts (Rāmāyaṇa + Yoga Sūtras) versus the ~4.8M-token Digital Corpus of Sanskrit.

Chapter 8 · The Frank AssessmentPaper? Patent?

A paper — plausible, with conditions

The field is active: character-level segmenters (Hellwig & Nehrdich 2018), lattice-transformer splitters (TransLIST 2022), and a multi-task ByT5-Sanskrit (2024) that is state-of-the-art on segmentation and morphology. A “we trained a Sanskrit model” paper is therefore not novel. What is a defensible angle here:

novel
Verification-survival as a first-class metric. Published systems report accuracy; none make “% of outputs a Pāṇinian engine can independently re-derive” the headline number. That's a guaranteed-faithfulness story, directly relevant to the hallucination literature.
novel
The compute-frugality claim. “A 10.7M model inside a symbolic lattice retains X% of a 300M model's accuracy at 1/30,000th the training cost” — if the numbers hold, that's a clean, quantified result about where capacity actually matters. Nobody has published the small end of this curve for Sanskrit.
required
Numbers on the standard benchmarks (SIGHUM/Hackathon splits, DCS) — otherwise reviewers cannot place the work. Roadmap steps 1–4 are exactly the missing ingredients.
required
The trigram-baseline ablation, plus a with/without-verifier ablation. The paper's story is the ablation table.

Realistic venues: the SIGHUM workshop at ACL (computational humanities — the natural home), LREC (resources), the World Sanskrit Conference computational track, or an *CL Findings paper if the frugality result is strong. Workshop first is the right ambition level: 2–3 months of the roadmap above, then write.

A patent — honestly, no

Three reasons. Prior art: neuro-symbolic propose-and-verify loops are extensively published; lattice-constrained decoding for Sanskrit specifically appears in existing papers. Eligibility: post-Alice, pure algorithm/pipeline claims with no novel hardware face steep abstract-idea rejections. Economics: $15–30k+ prosecution cost against a market with no plausible infringer paying royalties. If protection-of-record matters, publishing (paper or even this repo) is itself a defensive publication — it prevents anyone else from patenting the idea, for free. The value here compounds as open science: papers, citations, and adoption by the Sanskrit-NLP community (Ambuda/vidyut ecosystem), not as IP.

One-paragraph summary

We built a complete, demoable neuro-symbolic Sanskrit engine: a five-minute-trainable 10.7M-parameter character GPT that proposes analyses, wrapped in Pāṇini-derived rule tables that enumerate what's legal beforehand and veto what's unverifiable afterwards. Verification-survival is 96.4%; segmentation (38.5%) is the known weak link with a designed fix. The path to a workshop paper is concrete — constrained decoding, the trigram ablation, vidyut-generated morphology data, standard benchmarks. A patent is not worth pursuing; open publication is.
