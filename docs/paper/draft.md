## Abstract

We build a neuro-symbolic pipeline for Sanskrit sandhi (compound/word-boundary) segmentation from a symbolic core — a 1,481-rule Pāṇinian sandhi engine, an 85,715-word corpus lexicon, and a 2,259-root dhātukośa — and a single 10.7M-parameter character-level GPT trained from scratch in minutes on consumer Apple-Silicon hardware. The design follows the standard neuro-symbolic prescription: the model *ranks* candidates that a symbolic rule lattice *proposes*, and every choice is round-tripped through the rule engine for verification. An ablation that holds the candidate pool fixed and swaps only the ranking function shows this design does not work as intended: the neural ranker (6.8% exact-match) is statistically indistinguishable from a pure lexicon-plus-log-frequency scorer with no learned component at all (6.8%), and both merely saturate the ceiling set by what the rule lattice can propose in the first place — only 6.8% of held-out gold splits are reachable through the lattice at all, because 76.4% of real word boundaries are zero-change phonological abutments that leave no rule-output signature for a rewrite-rule lattice to key on. The identical checkpoint, used instead as a free autoregressive *generator*, reaches 39.8% exact-match and 58.0% word-F1 — six times the entire symbolic pipeline's ceiling — and 91.8% of its correct segmentations fall on inputs whose gold split the lattice pool provably cannot contain, direct evidence that the relevant knowledge already lived in the parameters and the rank-over-lattice architecture was discarding it. This gain has a nominal verification cost — 30% of free-decode outputs are not *re-derivable* by concatenating the predicted words back into the input — but closer analysis shows 70% of that 30% are the model correctly applying a real phonological rule and merely failing a strict verbatim-concatenation check, with only 1.5% of all outputs showing gross corruption. We resolve the tension directly: a copy-constrained decoder, which must replay the input's characters verbatim and may only *insert* boundary markers (never restricted to lattice-licensed junctures, since the ablation shows that restriction would recreate the 6.8% ceiling), reaches 40.8% exact-match and 57.9% word-F1 with 100% re-derivability by construction — matching free generation's coverage while eliminating its verification failure outright. Because the ranking, free-generation, and constrained-generation arms are the *same* 10.7M weights under three different inference procedures, the ~6x gap between the ranking and generation arms cannot be a capacity story; it is architectural, and lattice-constrained (here, copy-constrained) decoding is the synthesis that recovers both properties a neuro-symbolic pipeline is supposed to provide: generation's coverage and the symbolic path's verifiability.

## 1. Introduction

Sanskrit word segmentation is the flagship difficulty of Sanskrit computational linguistics: adjacent words in a verse or a compound are phonologically fused by sandhi (euphonic combination) rules that are productive, ambiguous, and — because Sanskrit famously runs Devanagari or transliterated text with no orthographic word boundaries at all inside a sentence — inseparable from the segmentation problem itself. A reader (human or machine) must simultaneously decide *where* the word boundaries are and *what phonological rule*, if any, obscures each one. Two families of systems have historically attacked this: rule-driven, lexicon-directed engines that enumerate every Pāṇini-licensed segmentation of a string (the Sanskrit Heritage Reader; Huet, 2003, 2009 — *citation TODO: confirm exact venue/year*), and, in the last decade, statistical and neural models trained end-to-end on the Digital Corpus of Sanskrit (DCS; Hellwig) to directly predict segmentations (Hellwig and Nehrdich, 2018; Krishna et al., 2018, 2020).

Our project sits at the intersection, and is explicit in its commitment to a *neuro-symbolic* design: "Neural proposes, Pāṇini disposes" is the governing principle of the larger system this model serves (a from-scratch Prakriyā Engine with its own sandhi, dhātu, and chandas modules). Concretely, a symbolic sandhi lattice enumerates every rule-licensed way to cut a sandhied string into words; a lexicon gate scores each candidate by whether its words are attested in a real corpus; and a small neural model was intended to supply the *ranking* signal that neither of those symbolic components can: a sense of which segmentation is not just rule-legal and lexically real, but *likely*, informed by distributional patterns the rule table and raw frequency counts do not capture.

This paper reports what happened when we tested that assumption directly. We built an ablation harness (`evals/baseline.py`, `evals/eval.py --ab`) that holds the candidate pool exactly fixed — the same rule-lattice output, generated once per input — and swaps out only the scoring function used to rank it: no scorer beyond raw lexicon coverage and log-frequency ("symbolic"), a character-trigram language model trained on the same corpus ("trigram"), and the GPT's own teacher-forced sequence log-probability ("neural"). A fourth arm removes the pool constraint entirely and lets the same GPT checkpoint decode a segmentation freely ("generate"). Once that ablation diagnosed *why* generation outperforms ranking (§6), we built and measured a fifth arm that acts on the diagnosis directly: a copy-constrained decoder ("constrained", §3.5) that must replay the input's characters verbatim and may only insert boundary markers, so its output is re-derivable by construction rather than by luck.

The result is a genuine surprise, and the paper's contribution is diagnosing *why* it happens rather than merely reporting it:

1. **As a ranker, the model adds nothing.** Neural-rank ties the pure lexicon+frequency baseline exactly (6.8% exact-match, 27/27 held-out examples where the gold answer is even reachable) and modestly beats a naive char-trigram (6.2%). All three rank arms are capped by the same **6.8% oracle pool ceiling** — the fraction of held-out gold segmentations that exist anywhere in the lattice's candidate list. A better ranker cannot rank in a candidate that was never proposed. We trace this ceiling to its mechanical cause: the lattice can only place a boundary where some rule's output left a detectable transformation signature in the string, and 76.4% of real word boundaries in our held-out set are *zero-change* abutments with no such signature — the lattice is a sandhi-transform detector, not a segmenter, and is 12.6x more likely to contain the gold split when a real transform is present (23.9%) than when the boundary is pure abutment (1.9%) (§6.1).
2. **As a generator, the same weights are transformative.** Free decoding from the identical checkpoint reaches 39.8% exact-match / 58.0% word-F1 — because generation is not restricted to the lattice's transition set, and can therefore place a zero-change word boundary the lattice cannot propose, and in general compose boundary decisions the finite rule table under-covers. This is not incidental: 91.8% of the generator's 159 exact wins fall on rows whose gold segmentation the lattice pool provably cannot contain (§6.2) — the clearest available evidence that the ranking arm's ceiling was never a knowledge gap in the model.
3. **The nominal verification cost is real but smaller than it first looks.** 30% of the generator's outputs are not *re-derivable* — concatenating the predicted words does not reproduce the input string. A finer-grained error analysis (§6.3) shows 70% of those cases are the model correctly applying a genuine phonological rule (e.g. an anusvāra/visarga alternation) and merely failing a strict verbatim-concatenation check, not fabricating content; only 1.5% of all 400 outputs show gross corruption (edit distance $\geq 6$ from the input).
4. **Capacity is not the bottleneck.** The same 10.7M parameters, the same training run, the same corpus: the delta between arms (2) and (1) is *entirely* attributable to what the model is allowed to do with what it learned, not to how much it learned. Extrapolating the rule table to its logical limit reinforces this: it already contains 438 identity rules, and a full identity closure over every phoneme pair would over-license candidate boundary points by 11.4x relative to the ~1.9 true boundaries per row — moving the bottleneck from proposal to ranking without raising the ceiling (§6.1). Scale cannot fix a design that discards the signal before the scorer ever runs.
5. **The architectural synthesis is implemented and measured, not proposed.** A copy-constrained decoder — must emit the input's characters verbatim, may only insert `' | '` boundary markers, never restricted to lattice-licensed junctures — reaches 40.8% exact-match and 57.9% word-F1 (statistically tied with free generation) at 100% re-derivability by construction (§5, §6.4). This completes the arc: ranker (no value added) → free generator (coverage, no guarantee) → constrained generator (coverage *and* guarantee).

We report this as an honest result set, not a leaderboard number: the segmentation exact-match rates here (6.2–40.8%) are well below published state-of-the-art Sanskrit segmenters (§2, §7), because this is a from-scratch, single-corpus, minutes-of-training model whose purpose is to isolate an *architectural* question, not to compete on accuracy. We think the ablation methodology — hold the pool fixed, swap only the scorer, remove the pool and let the same weights generate, then constrain generation to guarantee what ranking guaranteed for free — is the paper's most reusable contribution, independent of the specific accuracy numbers.

## 2. Related Work

**Rule- and lexicon-driven segmentation.** The Sanskrit Heritage Reader (Huet) is the reference implementation of lexicon-directed, Pāṇini-rule-licensed segmentation: it enumerates every phase- and sandhi-consistent split of an input and lets a human (or a downstream ranker) choose among them. Our symbolic lattice (§3) is a much smaller-scale descendant of the same idea — enumerate everything the rules permit, rank afterward — and our central finding (§5–6) is in effect a quantitative argument for why the *ranking* half of that architecture is the hard, and currently unsolved, part, at least at our scale and coverage.

**Statistical and neural segmentation.** Krishna et al. (2017) constructed a segmentation dataset from the Digital Corpus of Sanskrit (DCS; Hellwig) by aligning DCS's split points with an inventory of morphologically valid analyses; this dataset underlies most subsequent neural work. Krishna et al. (2018) frame segmentation and morphological tagging jointly as an energy-based, graph-parsing-style structured prediction problem over that dataset, later generalized to a broader graph-based framework covering segmentation, tagging, dependency parsing, linearization, and prosodification (Krishna et al., 2020). Hellwig and Nehrdich (2018) instead train end-to-end character-level recurrent/convolutional sequence models directly on raw-to-segmented text pairs, with no external lexicon or rule table — the architectural opposite pole from a lattice-constrained ranker, and, notably, closer in spirit to our unconstrained *generate* arm than to our *rank* arms. Both lines report large improvements over classical baselines on DCS-derived benchmarks; we make no accuracy comparison to them here (see Limitations, §8) because our held-out set is a single-corpus (Ramāyaṇa) split, not the standard DCS/SIGHUM Hackathon split these numbers are computed on.

**Sanskrit-scale pretrained models.** Nehrdich et al. (2024), *ByT5-Sanskrit*, train a single byte-level ByT5-family encoder-decoder (ByT5-small class, on the order of 300M parameters) with span-corruption pretraining over ~5.4B IAST-byte characters, fine-tuned across segmentation, OCR correction, and dependency parsing, reporting state-of-the-art or near-state-of-the-art results across tasks with one unified model. This is close to the scale-up target named in our own design spec (§4, Table 1's "300M ByT5-Sanskrit" comparator) and is the natural next rung above the 10.7M-parameter model in this paper; our contribution is not to compete with it on accuracy but to argue, from a controlled ablation at 30x-smaller scale and orders of magnitude less compute, that *how* a model of any size is embedded in the inference procedure (ranker vs. generator vs. constrained generator) determines whether its capacity is usable at all for this task.

**Tooling.** `vidyut` (Ambuda project; Prasad et al., ongoing — *citation TODO: confirm canonical reference, this is an actively developed open-source project rather than a single paper*) is a modern Rust/Python toolkit providing a fast Pāṇinian word generator and a segmentation/analysis pipeline; it is the natural production-grade replacement for the from-scratch `SandhiEngine`/`DhatuKosha` symbolic modules used here once this project's morphology (rather than just segmentation) ambitions mature (see `path-a-sanskrit-model-spec.md` §2.1, "the unfair advantage").

**Hallucination and verifiability in generation.** Our "re-derivability" metric — does concatenating the model's predicted words reproduce the input string — is a segmentation-specific instance of a broader theme in generation research: outputs that are fluent but not *grounded* in, or reconstructible from, their input are a known failure mode across summarization, translation, and open-domain QA, generically termed hallucination. We are not aware of prior work applying a literal re-concatenation check as a hallucination proxy specifically for Sanskrit (or other) segmentation, and flag the connection as the paper's bridge to that literature rather than a direct empirical comparison (*citations TODO: a representative hallucination/faithfulness survey should be added here*).

**Constrained decoding.** Our synthesis (§3.5, §6.4) — restrict a neural decoder's transitions at each step so its output is guaranteed to stand in a licensed relationship to the input, rather than either ranking a pre-enumerated pool or decoding unconstrained — is an instance of grammar-/automaton-constrained decoding, used elsewhere for code generation and finite-state-constrained NLG (*citations TODO: cite representative constrained-decoding work*). Our implementation is a *copy*-constrained decoder specifically (the model may replay input characters or open a boundary, nothing else), which is a deliberately weaker constraint than restricting transitions to sandhi-lattice edges; §6.1 and §6.4 explain why the lattice-licensed version would recreate the very ceiling this paper diagnoses. To our knowledge, copy-constrained decoding for the specific purpose of guaranteeing re-derivability in Pāṇinian sandhi segmentation has not been reported elsewhere; we present it here as a measured result, not a proposal.

## 3. System

### 3.1 Corpus and symbolic resources

The gold corpus is drawn from two sources, transliterated from Devanagari to SLP1 (an ASCII, one-character-per-phoneme encoding standard in computational Sanskrit) and cached once (`slm/corpus.py`):

- **Rāmāyaṇa**: 33,128 ślokas, yielding 308,392 word (pada) tokens in original word order — the source of *gold, human-segmented* word boundaries used for the segmentation task.
- **Yoga Sūtras**: 495 already-sandhied lines — used for denoising, not segmentation supervision.

The corpus vocabulary — every distinct pada observed, with corpus frequency — has 85,715 entries and is the lexicon gate described in §3.3.

Three symbolic engines (`slm/rules.py`) provide the rule-governed core:

- **`SandhiEngine`**: 1,481 first-syllable + second-syllable → result junction rules (`sandhi-rules-full.csv`), each carrying an explicit sūtra citation (e.g. `6.1.101 akaḥ savarṇe dīrghaḥ`, `8.3.15 kharavasānayor visarjanīyaḥ`). `.join(a, b)` applies forward sandhi; `.split(text)` performs a DFS over every position where some rule's result-string matches a substring of the input, enumerating every rule-licensed segmentation as an ordered candidate list.
- **`DhatuKośa`**: 2,259 dhātupāṭha (verbal root) entries, merged with a 294-root hand-curated table giving a clean, anubandha-stripped root form for exact-match verification.
- **`ChandasEngine`**: 145 named fixed-pattern (samavṛtta) meters, plus rule-defined recognition of anuṣṭubh — the epic śloka meter, whose pathyā/vipulā family is defined by position-wise laghu/guru constraints rather than one fixed pattern, so it needs its own recognizer rather than a lookup table entry.

### 3.2 The model

The neural component is a 10.7M-parameter decoder-only, nanoGPT-style Transformer (`slm/model.py`): 6 layers, 6 heads, 384-dimensional embeddings, causal self-attention via `scaled_dot_product_attention`, tied input/output embeddings, trained over a character-level SLP1 vocabulary (~120 ids including control tokens). Every task in the training mixture — root-to-morphology (`morph`), pada-pair-to-joined-form (`sandhi`), sandhied-line-to-segmented-padas (`seg`), weight-pattern-to-meter-name (`meter`), and span-denoising (`denoise`) — is framed as one causal sequence `<task> src <sep> tgt <eos>`, with loss taken only on the `tgt` span, so that a plain decoder-only LM learns the same seq2seq maps an encoder-decoder architecture would need for these tasks (the project's design spec, `path-a-sanskrit-model-spec.md`, targets a ByT5-style encoder-decoder at ~120–300M parameters as the eventual scale-up; this GPT is the "you can actually play" harness that trains in minutes on Apple-Silicon MPS rather than requiring a multi-GPU multi-day run).

The reported checkpoint reaches **val_bpb = 0.489** (bits-per-byte on held-out data; re-measured at 0.4889 in the run underlying Table 2, §6) after a training budget on the order of minutes on a single consumer Apple-Silicon Mac. For context, this is roughly 30x fewer parameters and orders of magnitude less compute than the ByT5-Sanskrit model described in §2.

### 3.3 The neuro-symbolic pipeline

The production `Analyzer.segment()` (`slm/pipeline.py`) implements the four-layer design named in the project's system spec (§4 of `path-a-sanskrit-model-spec.md`):

1. **Lattice** (symbolic): `SandhiEngine.split(text)` enumerates up to 20 rule-licensed candidate segmentations.
2. **Lexicon** (symbolic): each candidate's words are checked against the 85,715-word corpus vocabulary.
3. **Model** (neural): the GPT's teacher-forced log-probability of the candidate under the `<seg>` task scores it; the model's own greedy decode is *also* injected into the candidate pool as an extra proposal (in the production pipeline, though not in the controlled ablation of §4, where this would confound the pool across arms).
4. **Verify** (symbolic): each chosen pada is checked against the `DhatuKośa` (full morphological round-trip verification, e.g. against `vidyut-prakriya`, is future work — see `path-a-sanskrit-model-spec.md` §2.1 and §8 below).

Candidates are scored by a fixed linear combination:

$$
\text{score}(p) = 4.0 \cdot \text{cov}(p) + 0.5 \cdot \text{freq}_w(p) - 0.6 \cdot \text{oov}(p) + 0.15 \cdot \ell(p) - 0.05 \cdot n(p)
$$

where, for a candidate word sequence $p = (p_1, \dots, p_n)$: $\text{cov}(p)$ is the fraction of $p_i$ attested in the corpus vocabulary (the dominant, symbolic *gate* term); $\text{freq}_w(p)$ is the mean log-frequency of the $p_i$, normalized to $[0,1]$ (a finer-grained tie-break among equally-covered candidates, so a genuine high-frequency split beats one containing a rare word that merely happens to be in-vocabulary); $\text{oov}(p)$ is the raw count of out-of-vocabulary words (a hard per-word penalty against whole-string non-splits); $\ell(p)$ is the model's length-normalized log-probability of the candidate under `<seg>`; and $n(p)$ is the number of words (a mild bias against over-splitting). The coefficients encode a deliberate hierarchy — lexicon coverage dominates at 4.0, frequency is the primary tie-break at 0.5, and the *neural term sits last*, at 0.15, below both symbolic terms. This is not an oversight: it is exactly the ordering the ablation in §4–6 justifies after the fact, but it was arrived at empirically (§3.4) before the ablation existed to confirm it.

A further piece of engineering is necessary to make the lexicon gate function correctly at all: `SandhiEngine.split()` returns pada-finals in Pāṇinian **underlying** form (e.g. `rāmas`, `prāpnuyur`) exactly as encoded in `sandhi-rules-full.csv`, while the corpus vocabulary stores **surface (pausa)** forms (e.g. `rāmaḥ`). Word-final `s` or `r` in an underlying form always becomes visarga `ḥ` in pausa (utterance-final) position — sūtra 8.3.15, *kharavasānayor visarjanīyaḥ* — and since every candidate pada is by construction utterance-final within its own segmentation, this rewrite is always the licensed one. `Analyzer` therefore looks up every candidate word under both its literal and pausa-normalized form before deciding it is out-of-vocabulary; without this step, correct splits such as `rāmo'sti → rāmas | asti` score as if `rāmas` were an unknown word, and lose to spurious wrong splits.

### 3.4 Two bugs that had to be fixed before the ablation could be trusted

Two systems issues, uncovered while building this evaluation, are worth reporting as engineering findings in their own right, because either one would have silently invalidated the ablation in §4–6 by starving the candidate pool of the correct answer before any scorer got a chance to rank it.

**Skip-before-match DFS ordering.** `SandhiEngine.split()`'s original implementation explored rule-match branches of its search before the "skip this character, no rule applies here" branch. For any input containing an early spurious rule match — a substring that happens to coincide with some sandhi rule's result even though no real word boundary exists there — this ordering filled the candidate list's cap with combinatorially over-split garbage before the correct, *minimal*-boundary-count segmentation was ever generated. The fix reorders the DFS to explore the skip branch first, so minimal-boundary splits enter the pool before the cap is reached; downstream re-ranking (the lexicon/frequency/model score) is unaffected in principle, since it re-scores the whole returned pool regardless of order, but a candidate that never enters a length-capped pool can never be chosen no matter how it would have scored.

**Missing identity rules for a whole phoneme class.** `sandhi-rules-full.csv` encoded identity (no-change) junctures for word-final `r`, `s`, and `n` before vowels, but had no identity rows for word-final `m` before vowels — because sūtra 8.3.23 (*mo'nusvāraḥ*) only converts final `m` to anusvāra before a *consonant* (hal), leaving it unchanged before a vowel, a case the CSV's rule inventory simply never enumerated. This made zero-change boundaries like `kam | arcantaḥ` structurally unproposable by the lattice: no rule row existed whose "no transformation" case covered this juncture, so `.split()` could never emit it as a candidate, regardless of scorer quality. We added 13 identity rows covering `m` + each vowel. Both fixes are locked down by regression tests (`tests/test_split.py`, 6 cases; `tests/test_analyzer.py`, tie-break and pausa-normalization cases).

We flag this not as incidental cleanup but as the paper's most concrete illustration of §6's thesis: even after these fixes, the lattice remains a *finite, rule-enumerated* transition set, and any juncture pattern not yet represented as a CSV row — however common in real text — is invisible to every ranking arm no matter how good the ranker is. The free-generation arm (§4, §6) is not bound by this enumeration at all, which is a large part of why it escapes the 6.8% ceiling.

### 3.5 Copy-constrained decoding (`Inference.seg_constrained`)

The ablation in §4–6 diagnoses two complementary failure modes: the rank arms are trustworthy (every candidate is a verified, lattice-licensed parse of the input) but coverage-starved (§6.1); free generation is coverage-rich but occasionally untrustworthy (§6.3). `Inference.seg_constrained()` (`slm/infer.py`) is built directly against that diagnosis, and is deliberately *not* what the project's original design spec asked for.

The spec (`path-a-sanskrit-model-spec.md` §4) calls for "constrained beam search over the sandhi lattice... the model may only emit splits that exist as lattice edges." Our ablation shows why implementing that literally would be self-defeating: the lattice only proposes a boundary where some rule's *transformation* left a signature in the string (§6.1), so restricting the decoder to lattice edges would recreate the same 6.8%-ceiling blindness to zero-change junctures that limits the rank arms in the first place — the *symbolic edges themselves*, not just the up-front enumeration of them, are the bottleneck for the 76.4% of boundaries that involve no transformation at all.

We therefore constrain the decoder to a strictly weaker, but sufficient, invariant: **the output must be a copy of the input's characters, with `' | '` boundary markers freely insertable between them.** Concretely, at each input position the model's full next-token distribution is computed as usual, but only two outcomes are legal: emit the next literal input character (continue the current pada), or emit a boundary (`' | '`) if $P(\text{boundary})$ exceeds $P(\text{next input character})$ at that step — after which a `'|'` and a space are forced and an immediate second boundary (an empty pada) is forbidden. Every other token in the vocabulary is masked out at every step. The result concatenates back to the input by construction, for *any* input, with no reference to the sandhi lattice at all: **re-derivability is 100% by a structural guarantee, not an empirical rate.** `tests/test_constrained.py` locks this guarantee (re-derivability, non-empty padas) across seven cases including single-word and degenerate one-character inputs, deliberately *not* pinning the specific boundary decisions the model makes, since those are expected to drift with retraining while the structural guarantee must not.

This is a narrower guarantee than "every output is a Pāṇini-licensed sandhi transformation" — a copy-constrained decoder cannot, by design, ever propose a real phonological transformation at a boundary, since it may only copy or split, never rewrite. What it guarantees instead is the paper's weakest, most defensible verification property: the model's segmentation is always *some* literal parse of the string it was actually given, never a fabrication of characters that were not there. Whether that is the right guarantee to ship, versus a genuine lattice-licensed constrained decode that also captures true transformation boundaries, is discussed in §6.4.

## 4. Experiments

### 4.1 Task and held-out set

We evaluate on the `seg` task: given a continuous, sandhied SLP1 string, predict the sequence of underlying words. Training examples for this task (`slm/datagen.py: build_seg`) are constructed by taking 2–4 consecutive padas from a real Rāmāyaṇa śloka (preserving genuine word order and genuine adjacency), forward-applying the sandhi engine across the whole window to obtain a sandhied surface string, and supervising the split back to the original padas — so a meaningful fraction of held-out examples do carry a genuine phonological transformation at the seam (not merely a space to reinsert into unchanged text; see also the Limitations discussion in §8 on how this interacts with the re-derivability metric). We evaluate on 400 held-out `seg` examples from the model's val split (work-held-out relative to the corpus builder, at the pada-window level).

### 4.2 Arms

All five arms are computed by `evals/eval.py --ab`, backed by `evals/baseline.py` and `slm/infer.py`. The three **rank** arms share an *identical* candidate pool per example — `SandhiEngine.split(text, max_results=20)` plus the literal no-split fallback — generated once and reused across arms, so the comparison isolates the scoring function, never the candidate set:

- **symbolic**: score is the `Analyzer` formula (§3.3) with the neural term $\ell(p)$ fixed to 0 — lexicon coverage and log-frequency alone, no learned component whatsoever.
- **trigram**: identical formula, with $\ell(p)$ replaced by the log-probability of the joined candidate under an interpolated character trigram language model (add-$k$ smoothing, $k=0.1$; linear interpolation weights $0.7/0.2/0.1$ over trigram/bigram/unigram), trained from scratch on the *same* corpus the GPT saw (Rāmāyaṇa padas + Yoga Sūtra lines) — the "honesty baseline" the design spec explicitly calls for: a model with essentially no capacity or pretraining advantage over raw corpus statistics, to check whether the GPT's added complexity earns its keep over the simplest possible sequence model.
- **neural**: the full `Analyzer` formula with $\ell(p)$ the GPT's teacher-forced, length-normalized log-probability of the candidate under the `<seg>` task prompt — the production scorer.
- **generate**: the pool constraint is dropped entirely. The same GPT checkpoint greedily decodes a segmentation autoregressively (temperature $\to 0$, top-$k{=}1$) from the `<seg>` prompt with no reference to the lattice at all. Its output is post-processed identically to the other arms (split on `|`, strip whitespace) but is not drawn from, or restricted to, any symbolic candidate list.
- **constrained**: `Inference.seg_constrained()` (§3.5). Same checkpoint, same greedy decision rule at each step, but the vocabulary is masked at every position to just {next input character, boundary marker}, so the output is guaranteed re-derivable rather than merely observed to be.

### 4.3 Metrics

- **Exact-match**: predicted word sequence equals gold, verbatim.
- **Word-F1**: multiset (bag-of-words) F1 between predicted and gold word sequences, giving partial credit when some but not all words match — $F_1 = 2 \cdot \text{prec} \cdot \text{rec} / (\text{prec} + \text{rec})$ over multiset intersection counts.
- **Re-derivability**: does concatenating the predicted words exactly reproduce the input string? For the three rank arms, this is a *descriptive* statistic about how often the predicted (correct-or-not) split happens to require no phonological transformation at its boundaries — since lattice candidates are drawn from rule-licensed *underlying* forms, most of which differ from the literal surface substring at a sandhi seam by construction, non-concatenation for a rank-arm prediction is expected and not itself evidence of error (see §8 for the important caveat this raises for reading the generate arm's number the same way). For the **generate** arm specifically, where no lattice constrains the output at all, we use it as the paper's proxy for *hallucination*: an unconstrained decode that cannot even be reassembled into the input string it was supposedly segmenting has failed the weakest possible grounding check. For the **constrained** arm, re-derivability is not measured at all in the usual sense — it is a structural invariant of the decoding procedure (§3.5) and is reported as exactly 100% by construction.
- **Hit-given-pool**: exact-match accuracy restricted to the subset of examples where the gold segmentation is actually present somewhere in the candidate pool — i.e., ranking accuracy conditioned on the ranking problem being solvable at all. This isolates ranker quality from lattice coverage.
- **Oracle pool ceiling**: the fraction of held-out examples for which the gold segmentation appears anywhere in the lattice-generated candidate pool, under *any* scoring function whatsoever (an oracle that always picks correctly when the answer is present). No rank arm can exceed this number by construction.

### 4.4 Compute and reproducibility

The entire ablation — training-corpus trigram fit, 400-example evaluation across five arms — runs in a few minutes on a single consumer Apple-Silicon Mac (MPS backend), on the same 10.7M-parameter checkpoint used throughout this paper (val_bpb = 0.4889 at evaluation time). Run with `uv run python -m evals.eval --ab --n 400`.

### 4.5 Boundary-taxonomy and error-anatomy methodology

Section 6 draws on a supplementary quantitative analysis (`docs/paper/capacity-vs-architecture.md`, included as supplementary material with full scripts and verbatim run logs) built on top of the same 400-example held-out set and the same cached greedy-decode predictions. Two classification procedures underlie it:

- **Zero-change vs. transform boundary classification.** A gold boundary between adjacent padas $p_i, p_{i+1}$ is classified *zero-change* if the two padas abut verbatim in the input string at that position (equivalently, for a whole row, if concatenating all gold padas reproduces the input exactly); otherwise a real sandhi transform rewrote the junction. Per-boundary classification within a row uses greedy positional alignment of each pada against the input string.
- **Generation error categorization.** Each of the free-generate arm's non-re-derivable outputs is categorized by comparing its predicted character stream against the input: truncation (a strict prefix, shorter), extra/hallucinated span (longer, or containing a repeated substring), sandhi-transform-consistent (Levenshtein distance $\leq 2$ from the input, and not shorter — i.e., a small, localized edit at what is very plausibly a real phonological junction), or unclassified substitution/deletion (larger, non-length-increasing edits). Levenshtein edit distance between the predicted character stream and the input is additionally computed directly, independent of the categorical bucketing, as a continuous severity check.

A third procedure (**A5**, §6.4) tests whether the constrained decoder's shortfall against its own pre-registered projection is a decode-time artifact: a sweep of the boundary-opening decision threshold $\tau$ over $\{-3, -2, -1, -0.5, 0, 0.5, 1\}$ (re-implemented separately from the production `seg_constrained` to avoid touching shipped code, with $\tau=0$ verified to reproduce the production 40.8%/163-of-400 exactly as a harness sanity check), a width-4 length-normalized beam search over the same copy-vs-boundary decision (piloted on 100 rows before being promoted to the full 400), and a decomposition of exact-match by whether the free-decode arm had already re-derived each row cleanly or corrupted it.

All three procedures, their code, and their verbatim console output are reproduced in the supplementary document; we summarize only the results relevant to this paper's argument in §6.

## 5. Results

Table 1 reports the five-arm ablation on 400 held-out Rāmāyaṇa segmentation examples, re-run for this paper and matching the project's tracked ablation-gate numbers exactly.

**Table 1: Segmentation ablation, 400 held-out examples.**

| Arm | Exact | Word-F1 | Re-derivable | Hit \| Pool |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Symbolic (lexicon + log-freq) | 6.8% (27/400) | 19.5% | 31.8% | 27/27 |
| Char-trigram rank | 6.2% (25/400) | 19.1% | 31.8% | 25/27 |
| Neural rank (GPT logprob) | 6.8% (27/400) | 19.6% | 32.0% | 27/27 |
| Neural generate (GPT free decode) | 39.8% (159/400) | 58.0% | 70.0% | — |
| **Copy-constrained decode** | **40.8%** (163/400) | 57.9% | **100.0%** | — |
| Oracle pool ceiling | 6.8% (27/400) | — | — | — |

Four observations follow directly from the table.

**The neural ranker ties, and the trigram loses to, the pure-symbolic baseline.** Neural-rank and symbolic-rank are identical on exact-match (27/400) and separated by 0.1 points of word-F1 (19.6 vs. 19.5) — a difference well within noise at $n=27$ correct examples. The interpolated char-trigram, despite being trained on exactly the same corpus, does *worse* than raw lexicon-plus-frequency (25 vs. 27 exact), suggesting that for this candidate-selection problem, a smoothed local character-sequence model is actively somewhat *worse* than not scoring sequence likelihood at all, once lexicon coverage already does the heavy lifting.

**All three rank arms saturate the same ceiling.** The oracle pool ceiling — the best any ranker, however perfect, could achieve given this lattice — is 6.8% (27/400), identical to the symbolic and neural arms' raw exact-match. Hit-given-pool confirms this is not coincidence: symbolic and neural both get 27/27 right *when the gold answer is in the pool at all* (trigram gets 25/27). In other words, on this held-out set, whenever the correct segmentation exists anywhere in the candidate list, both non-trivial rank arms already find it. The ranking problem, restricted to this lattice, is close to *solved* by lexicon coverage alone; the bottleneck is entirely upstream, in what the lattice proposes (§6.1).

**Free generation blows through the ceiling by 6x, at a nominal verification cost.** The identical GPT checkpoint, decoding without any lattice constraint, reaches 39.8% exact-match and 58.0% word-F1 — respectively 5.9x and roughly 3x the rank arms' figures, and 5.9x the oracle ceiling those arms are capped by. This cannot be a capacity story (§6): it is the *same weights*. It is also not error-free: only 70.0% of its outputs are re-derivable by literal concatenation into the input string, meaning 30% of the time the model's free decode does not correspond to a literal parse of the input it was given. §6.3 shows this headline 30% is a coarser signal than it looks — most of it is the model correctly applying sandhi, not fabricating content — but the point stands that free decoding offers no *guarantee*, only an empirically high rate.

**Copy-constrained decoding matches or exceeds free generation on every metric that matters, with the guarantee free generation lacked.** The constrained arm reaches 40.8% exact-match — a full point *above* free generation, not merely on par with it — and 57.9% word-F1, statistically indistinguishable from free generation's 58.0% (a 0.1-point gap), while re-derivability moves from an empirical 70.0% to a structural 100.0%. This is the paper's central positive result: the same weights, given a decoding procedure whose legal moves are copy-input-character or open-boundary rather than emit-anything, do not lose accuracy for the added guarantee — if anything they gain a point, plausibly because the copy constraint removes an entire class of character-level errors (substitutions, drops, repeated spans) the free decoder was otherwise free to make. §6.4 examines this result against a pre-registered projection made before the constrained decoder was built.

## 6. Discussion: why the lattice ceiling exists, and where the model's knowledge lives

The central argument of this paper is a subtraction. Holding the model, the training data, the tokenization, and the checkpoint fixed, the only thing that changes across the rank / generate / constrained rows of Table 1 is *what the model is permitted to do with what it learned*: score a fixed, symbolically-enumerated list of at most 20 candidates; decode a sequence of characters with no list at all; or decode under a copy-plus-boundary constraint. The exact-match rate moves from 6.8% to 39.8% to 40.8%. This section works through why, using the supplementary quantitative analysis introduced in §4.5 (full methodology, scripts, and verbatim run logs in `docs/paper/capacity-vs-architecture.md`).

### 6.1 Failure taxonomy: the lattice is a transform detector, not a segmenter

`SandhiEngine.split()` (§3.1, §3.4) closes a pada only in its *rule-match* branch — when some rule's `result` string matches the input at the current position; its *skip* branch never creates a boundary, it only accumulates characters. Mechanically, then, a boundary is proposed **only where a sandhi transform left a detectable rule-output signature in the string.** We classified all 757 gold pada boundaries across the 400 held-out rows (§4.5) into zero-change abutments (the two padas meet with no phonological alternation at all) versus genuine transform junctures. The result: **578/757 (76.4%) of real boundaries are zero-change**, and in 312/400 rows (78.0%) *every* boundary in the row is zero-change. Three of every four real word boundaries in this corpus simply carry no sandhi signal for a rewrite-rule lattice to key on.

This directly explains the 6.8% pool ceiling. Conditioning on boundary type: the gold split is in the lattice pool for only 6/312 (1.9%) of all-zero-change rows, versus 21/88 (23.9%) of rows with at least one real transform — the lattice is **12.6x more likely** to contain the correct answer when a transform is present. Inspecting the 373 misses further: the lattice essentially always proposes *some* split (371/373; only 2 rows get no boundary at all) — the failure mode is not silence but **crowd-out**: with a fixed `max_results=20` and a mean/median pool size of 19.5/20, the lattice fills its budget with wrongly-placed candidate boundaries (mostly at spurious transform-like substrings) before the correct, transform-free split is ever reached, exactly the DFS pathology partially addressed in §3.4.

A natural objection is that this is simply an under-resourced rule table — add more identity (no-change) rules and the ceiling should rise. The data argues against this. The table already contains 438 identity rules out of 1,480 (30%) — these are precisely the "no phonological change happens here" rows like the 13 added in §3.4 — and the ceiling is still 6.8%. Extrapolating to the limit makes the argument sharp: across the 400 held-out rows there are 9,051 characters and only 757 true boundaries (a mean of 1.89 per row, out of a mean 22.6 characters); a *full* identity closure — an identity rule for every possible final-phoneme/initial-phoneme pair — would license a candidate boundary at essentially every one of the 8,651 inter-character gaps, an **11.4x over-licensing ratio**. Doing this would not recover the gold split; it would convert an unsolvable *proposal* problem (the boundary isn't offered) into an already-unsolved *ranking* problem (choose ~1.9 correct cut points out of ~21.6 offered) — precisely the selection task the rank arms already handle only as well as raw lexicon coverage allows (§5). Adding rules moves the bottleneck, it does not lift the ceiling: the lattice-then-rank architecture is the wrong shape for zero-change junctures, not merely under-resourced for them.

### 6.2 Capacity probe: does the model already know what ranking discards?

If the neural component's *knowledge* of Sanskrit segmentation were the limiting factor on the ranking arm, we would expect a smarter model to rank better *and* generate better, in some rough proportion — the ranker should at least partially track the generator's improvement. Instead, of the free-generation arm's 159 exact wins, **146 (91.8%) are on rows whose gold split the lattice pool provably cannot contain** — i.e., on inputs where no ranker, however perfect, could ever have selected the correct answer, because it was never a candidate. Only 14 of the 27 gold-in-pool rows are ones where generation happened to miss what ranking could in principle have rescued; taking the union of "generation got it exactly right" and "gold was in the rankable pool" reaches a hybrid ceiling of 173/400 (43.2%), still dominated by generation's contribution. The only coherent reading is that **the model's useful segmentation knowledge was present all along**, and the ranking arm never had a task on which that knowledge could matter: once lexicon coverage has picked out the small set of candidates the lattice bothered to propose, there is essentially nothing left for a sequence-scoring term to decide, because the lattice's own enumeration (§6.1) already discarded, before any scorer ran, the large majority of segmentations a genuinely capable model would have wanted to consider. A ranker cannot rank in what was never on the ballot — and per §6.1, for 93.2% of these held-out rows, the correct answer never was.

This has a direct, practical implication for where compute should be spent. The project's own design spec treats this GPT as a stepping stone to a ~120–300M-parameter ByT5-style scale-up (§2, §3.2) — the same scale as the published ByT5-Sanskrit model (Nehrdich et al., 2024). Scaling *the ranker* along this axis, while holding the rank-then-verify architecture fixed, would likely buy little: the bottleneck is lattice coverage, not ranker quality, and a 300M-parameter ranker scoring the same 20-candidate, structurally-blind pool would still be capped near 6.8% for the same reason a bigger calculator does not help when the numbers fed into it are already wrong. The generation result shows the opposite: model quality matters enormously once the architecture lets that quality act on a wide-enough space of outputs.

### 6.3 Generation error anatomy: what the non-re-derivable 30% actually contains

The free-generate arm's 30% non-re-derivability rate (§5) is real, but a finer look shows it overstates the failure. Of the 120 non-re-derivable outputs, we categorized each by comparing its predicted character stream against the input (§4.5): **84 (70.0%) are within a small edit distance ($\leq 2$) of the input and not shorter than it** — consistent with the model correctly applying a real, local phonological rule (an anusvāra/visarga alternation, most commonly) and failing only the strict verbatim-concatenation check, not fabricating content. Only 26 (21.7%) show an extra or repeated span consistent with genuine hallucination, and 10 (8.3%) are unclassified substitutions or drops; truncation never occurs. Looking at raw edit distance directly: 76 of the 120 are edit-distance 1 from the input (66 of those a single-character anusvāra/visarga swap), and only **6 rows out of all 400 (1.5%)** show edit distance $\geq 6$ — the threshold we treat as *gross* corruption. Read plainly: the strict concatenation check is a useful, cheap proxy, but it is not itself a hallucination measurement, and the true rate of gross, uninterpretable corruption in free generation on this held-out set is closer to 1.5% of all outputs than to the 30% the raw re-derivability number suggests on its own. We nonetheless keep re-derivability, not this finer breakdown, as the paper's headline number for the free-generate arm, precisely because the finer breakdown requires a post-hoc classifier that could itself be wrong (see §8) — which is exactly the argument for the structural guarantee in §6.4 over a post-hoc filter.

### 6.4 Copy-constrained decoding as the synthesis — projected, then measured

The project's design spec (`path-a-sanskrit-model-spec.md` §4) specifies constrained beam search over the sandhi lattice as the target segmentation architecture: "the model may only emit splits that exist as lattice edges... Model = ranker over symbolically valid paths, never a free generator." §6.1 shows why implementing that literally would be self-defeating here: lattice edges are transform signatures, and 76.4% of real boundaries carry no such signature, so restricting decoding to lattice edges would reproduce the rank arms' 6.8% blindness to zero-change junctures — the very failure mode this section diagnoses. The synthesis we build and measure instead constrains decoding to a strictly weaker but sufficient invariant: copy the input, insert boundaries (§3.5).

Before building it, we made a falsifiable prediction from the free-generation data: restricting the model to its own re-derivable ("clean") subset of predictions — 280 of 400 rows — and asking what fraction of *those* were already exact matches gives 134/280 = 47.9%. Since a copy-constrained decoder is re-derivable everywhere by construction, projecting that clean-subset exact-match rate onto the full 400 rows gives a **47.9% projected ceiling**, an anticipated +8.1-point gain over free generation's raw 39.8%. The measured result, once we built `seg_constrained` (§3.5) and ran it on the same 400 rows, is **40.8%** (Table 1) — 7.1 points below the projection.

We then asked, directly, where the 7.1 points went, rather than leaving the gap as a qualitative guess (**A5**, following the supplementary analysis's A1–A4, §4.5; full methodology and scripts in `docs/paper/capacity-vs-architecture.md`). Two candidate explanations were tested and both were **refuted**. First, a copy-bias hypothesis: perhaps the greedy per-step comparison ($P(\text{boundary})$ vs. $P(\text{next character})$) is miscalibrated toward copying, so a threshold $\tau$ favoring boundaries more readily should help. A sweep over $\tau$ shows a clean single peak exactly at the production decoder's implicit $\tau=0$ (40.8%/57.9%); moving *either* direction — more boundary-eager ($\tau<0$: 40.0% at $-0.5$, collapsing to 24.5% at $-3$) or more conservative ($\tau>0$: 39.2% at $+0.5$) — strictly hurts. The shipped greedy rule is already the calibrated optimum, not an under-tuned one. Second, a search-depth hypothesis: perhaps greedy decoding commits to locally-optimal but globally-wrong boundary choices that a wider search would avoid. A width-4, length-normalized beam search recovers only **+1.0 point** (41.8% exact, 58.8% F1) at roughly 10x the wall-clock cost (629 ms/row vs. greedy) — real, but nowhere near the 6.1 points still separating it from the 47.9% projection.

A direct decomposition explains why the projection was optimistic rather than wrong on its own terms: splitting the 400 rows by whether the *free*-decode arm (§6.3) already re-derived them cleanly (280 rows) or corrupted them (120 "hard" rows), the constrained-greedy decoder scores **exactly** 47.9% (134/280) on the clean subset — the projection's basis holds precisely where it was measured — but only **24.2%** (29/120) on the hard subset. Of the 29-row shortfall from the projected 192 exact matches (47.9% $\times$ 400) down to the measured 163, 28 rows are attributable to the hard-row deficit and 0 to the clean-row deficit. **The gap is not a decoding-search problem; it is a modeling problem specific to a subset of genuinely hard rows** — rows where the free decoder was already corrupting characters turn out to be exactly the rows where the constrained decoder also struggles to place boundaries correctly, because copy-constraining the character stream does nothing to help the model *decide where to cut* an input it does not represent well. Neither better calibration nor a wider search closes that gap; more supervision or representational capacity aimed specifically at those hard rows would need to.

This sharpens, rather than undermines, the paper's central claim: copy-constrained decoding still matches or exceeds free generation's coverage (40.8% vs. 39.8% at greedy; 41.8% with beam-4) while converting its 70.0% empirical re-derivability rate into a 100.0% structural guarantee — completing the arc from ranker (no value added, §5) to free generator (coverage without guarantee, §5, §6.2–6.3) to constrained generator (coverage *and* guarantee, measured here). We recommend the greedy $\tau=0$ decoder (40.8%) as the shipped operating point — beam-4's +1.0 point does not obviously justify a 10x latency cost for most applications — and note that A5's finding narrows exactly where future effort belongs (§6.5).

### 6.5 Architecture first, then capacity

Read across §5–6.4, the investigation traces exactly one lever at each stage, and the lever changes as soon as the previous one is exhausted. At the ranking stage, capacity was provably irrelevant: a 10.7M-parameter neural scorer could not be distinguished from a zero-parameter lexicon-frequency scorer (§5), because both were bounded by lattice coverage, not model quality (§6.1–6.2) — no amount of additional training or scale would have moved the 6.8% ceiling, because the ceiling was never a modeling problem. Fixing the *architecture* — copy-constrained decoding in place of rank-over-lattice — resolved that bottleneck outright and, for free, recovered the model's latent segmentation knowledge (§6.2–6.4). Only *after* that architectural fix is in place, and only once decode-search options (calibrated threshold, beam search) are exhausted and refuted as explanations for the residual gap (§6.4), does the investigation arrive at a point where more training data or model capacity is the indicated next lever — and even there, narrowly: not "scale the model" in general, but specifically "improve boundary-placement supervision on the subset of inputs the model already struggles to represent" (the 120 hard rows, §6.4). We think this ordering is the paper's most transferable methodological lesson, independent of Sanskrit or segmentation specifically: **architecture first, then capacity** — check whether a pipeline's stage boundaries are discarding a model's usable signal before spending compute on a bigger model to produce more signal for that stage to discard.

## 7. Compute-frugality note

We flag as a secondary contribution, separate from the ranking-vs-generation argument, that all of the numbers in Table 1 — including a from-scratch character-level GPT that *outperforms its own symbolic pipeline sixfold as a generator, and does so with a full verification guarantee once copy-constrained* — come from a 10.7M-parameter model trained in minutes on a single consumer Apple-Silicon machine, no cluster, no accelerator beyond what ships in a laptop. The comparator scale named in this project's own design spec, and realized externally by Nehrdich et al. (2024)'s ByT5-Sanskrit, is roughly 30x larger and trained on billions of characters over a substantially longer compute budget. We do not claim our model is competitive with that system in absolute accuracy (see Limitations, §8) — it is not, and is not evaluated on the same benchmark. We do claim that the *entire* architectural arc in §6 — ranker adds nothing, generator has the knowledge, constrained generator has the knowledge *and* the guarantee — was measured entirely within a frugal, single-machine, single-corpus budget, including the falsifiable projection-then-measurement exercise in §6.4, and is therefore a cheap experiment worth running before committing to a much larger training run whose ranker-vs-generator-vs-constrained-generator behavior might otherwise be assumed rather than measured.

## 8. Limitations

We list these plainly, because the paper's claims are about architecture, and an architectural claim is only as strong as the evidence base it was measured against.

- **Single-corpus evaluation.** All segmentation numbers are on a held-out split of one corpus (Rāmāyaṇa epic verse); we have not evaluated on Vedic material, prose śāstra text, or any genre with substantially different compounding or sandhi-density patterns. The lattice-coverage bottleneck we diagnose (§3.4, §6) is corpus- and rule-table-specific; a rule table built or extended against a different genre could shift the 6.8% ceiling substantially in either direction.
- **No DCS / SIGHUM standard split comparison.** We do not evaluate on the Digital Corpus of Sanskrit or the SIGHUM Sanskrit Word Segmentation Hackathon splits that Hellwig and Nehrdich (2018) and the Krishna et al. line of work report against, so none of our exact-match/F1 numbers should be read as comparable to, or competitive with, published state-of-the-art segmentation accuracy. This paper's numbers are only meaningful *relative to each other* (arm vs. arm, on an identical pool), not in absolute terms against the literature. Adding a DCS-split evaluation is the most important piece of follow-up work for turning this from an architecture study into an accuracy claim.
- **The re-derivability metric is a coarse hallucination proxy, and we want to be explicit about its asymmetry — even after the error-anatomy pass in §6.3.** For the rank arms, "not re-derivable by literal concatenation" is *expected and benign*: a lattice candidate encodes a Pāṇinian *underlying* form related to the surface input by a licensed sandhi rule, not a literal substring of it, so failing to concatenate back to the input is simply evidence that a real phonological transformation happened at that seam — not an error signal. (This is why all three rank arms show near-identical ~32% re-derivability regardless of correctness: it is close to a fixed property of how often the *gold* segmentations in this held-out set happen to have zero-change boundaries, not a measure of ranker quality.) For the **generate** arm, §6.3's edit-distance-based categorization shows most of the 30% non-concatenating outputs (70% of them) are small, localized edits consistent with a legitimate sandhi transform rather than fabrication, with only ~1.5% of all outputs showing gross corruption — but that categorization is itself a heuristic (edit distance $\leq 2$ from the input), not a symbolic verification. We have not yet run the actual `SandhiEngine.join` verifier over those cases to confirm each small edit corresponds to a *real*, rule-licensed transformation rather than a coincidentally-close wrong answer, and consider this the most important immediate follow-up to turn an edit-distance proxy into a validated hallucination rate — filed as a **`rederiv_v2`** metric (re-derivability up to a symbolically-verified sandhi rewrite, not just literal concatenation) rather than shipped as a result in this draft. This is exactly why we do not rely on post-hoc classification as the paper's actual verification mechanism: **copy-constrained decoding** (§6.4) sidesteps the whole question by making every generated step structurally re-derivable in the first place, rather than requiring a downstream classifier — heuristic or symbolic — to sort correct transforms from genuine fabrications after the fact.
- **Gold segmentation is not purely boundary-insertion.** Training and evaluation examples for `seg` are built by forward-applying real sandhi rules across genuine consecutive Rāmāyaṇa padas (`slm/datagen.py: build_seg`), so a meaningful share of held-out seams do involve an actual phonological alternation, not merely whitespace reinsertion into already-separated text — the task is not trivial. §6.1 quantifies this precisely: 76.4% of the 757 gold boundaries in the held-out set are zero-change abutments and 23.6% are genuine transform junctures, so the two classes are both well represented but heavily skewed toward zero-change. We have not stratified *accuracy* (as opposed to boundary counts) by seam type per row — i.e., we do not yet report exact-match separately for all-zero-change rows versus rows with at least one transform — which would let us confirm directly, rather than infer from the pool-membership numbers in §6.1, that the generation and constrained arms' advantage over ranking is concentrated on zero-change seams specifically.
- **Segmentation-only ablation.** The five-arm ranking/generation/constrained comparison in this paper covers only the `seg` task. The pipeline's other tasks (morphological analysis, sandhi joining, meter identification, denoising) are trained in the same multi-task mixture and share the same checkpoint, but we have not run an equivalent ablation for them, so we do not know whether the architectural conclusion of §6 generalizes beyond segmentation.
- **Small sample size for the pool-conditioned metric.** Hit-given-pool is computed over only 27 examples (the oracle ceiling's numerator) — enough to distinguish 27/27 from 25/27 as a directional signal, but too small to support a strong statistical claim about the *size* of any gap between rank arms. We report it as suggestive, not conclusive, evidence that the neural and symbolic rankers are near-tied rather than meaningfully different.
- **The constrained decoder underperforms its own pre-registered projection by 7.1 points (§6.4), and closing that gap is now known to require data, not a smarter decoder.** We initially suspected the gap was a decode-search artifact (a miscalibrated greedy comparison, or a search-depth limitation); a follow-up ablation (**A5**, §6.4, §4.5) tested and refuted both. A $\tau$-sweep over the boundary-opening threshold peaks exactly at the shipped greedy rule's implicit $\tau=0$ (40.8%), so the decision rule is already calibrated, not under-tuned. A width-4 beam search recovers only +1.0 point (41.8%) at ~10x the wall-clock cost, still 6.1 points short of the projection. Decomposing by difficulty shows why: the constrained decoder hits the projection's basis *exactly* (47.9%, 134/280) on rows the free-decode arm already re-derived cleanly, but collapses to 24.2% (29/120) on the rows it corrupted — the same rows are hard for both character fidelity and boundary placement. The residual gap is therefore a **model-knowledge limitation on a specific hard-row subset**, not a decoding-algorithm limitation, and the natural next step is targeted segmentation supervision (or additional capacity) on that subset rather than further decode-time search — which we have not yet attempted, and flag as the paper's most concrete remaining open item.

## 9. Conclusion

We set out to test, empirically, a design assumption common to neuro-symbolic NLP pipelines: that a neural model earns its place by ranking candidates a symbolic system proposes, with the symbolic system supplying coverage and verifiability and the neural system supplying judgment. For Sanskrit sandhi segmentation, at the scale of a 10.7M-parameter character GPT trained in minutes against a 1,481-rule sandhi lattice and an 85,715-word lexicon, that assumption does not hold: the neural ranker is indistinguishable from a lexicon-and-frequency scorer with no learned parameters at all, because both are capped by the same lattice-coverage ceiling (6.8% of held-out examples) — a ceiling we trace mechanically to the fact that 76.4% of real word boundaries are zero-change phonological abutments a rewrite-rule lattice cannot detect, and cannot fix by adding more rules, since the rule table already holds 438 identity rules and a full identity closure would over-license boundary candidates 11.4x without raising the ceiling. The identical model, freed to generate rather than rank, reaches six times that ceiling, and 91.8% of its exact wins fall on inputs the lattice pool provably cannot cover — direct evidence the knowledge was in the parameters all along, not absent from them. That gain came with a nominal verification cost (30% non-re-derivability), which a finer error analysis shows is 70% legitimate sandhi transforms misclassified by a strict concatenation check, and only 1.5% genuine gross corruption. Because ranking, free generation, and constrained generation are the same weights under three inference procedures, the gap between them cannot be a capacity story; it is architectural — and we resolved it, not merely proposed resolving it: a copy-constrained decoder, deliberately *not* restricted to sandhi-lattice edges (§6.1, §6.4 explain why that restriction would recreate the ceiling), reaches 40.8% exact-match — matching or exceeding free generation — at 100% re-derivability by construction. This completes the paper's arc: ranker (no value added) → free generator (coverage, no guarantee) → constrained generator (coverage *and* guarantee), measured throughout on the same 10.7M-parameter checkpoint trained in minutes on a single consumer machine. The constrained decoder's measured 40.8% falls 7.1 points short of a projection made from the free-generate data before it was built (§6.4); we tested, rather than assumed, the two obvious explanations — a miscalibrated decision rule and insufficient search depth — and refuted both (a threshold sweep peaks exactly at the shipped rule; a 10x-costlier beam search recovers only +1.0 point). The gap instead isolates to a specific subset of genuinely hard rows where the model's boundary-placement knowledge, not the decoding procedure, is the limiting factor.

That last result places a natural cap on this paper's own argument, and we think it is the more useful ending for it: at every earlier stage of this investigation, *architecture* was the lever and *capacity* was a red herring — a bigger ranker could not have helped, because the ranking arm's ceiling was never a knowledge problem. Only once the architecture is fixed — copy-constrained decoding in place of rank-over-lattice — does the investigation reach a point where more supervision or capacity, aimed narrowly at the hard-row subset A5 isolates, becomes the indicated next step. **Architecture first, then capacity** (§6.5) is, we think, the transferable lesson: check whether a pipeline discards a model's usable signal before spending compute to produce more signal for it to discard, and only reach for scale once that architectural question has actually been asked and answered.
